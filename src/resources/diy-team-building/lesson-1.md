## The Team as a Socio-Technical System

A team is never just what meets the eye: people around a table, project goals, task assignments. A team is a socio-technical system. It consists of people with needs, expectations, and habits, and of technical structures like processes, roles, and tools. Dynamics emerge that cannot be controlled linearly: what helps one person may hinder another.

Two fundamental forces are always at play: the **social reality** of people — their communication, feelings, history — and the **technical structures** that enable or constrain collaboration. "Technical" here doesn't just mean digital: it includes role distributions, communication channels, timelines, tools, processes, job descriptions, and workspaces — everything that is formally designed or prescribed.

People in teams are not neutral. They observe each other, interpret behavior, sense implicit expectations. They act from trust, competition, connection, or uncertainty. This is where the tension begins: what is technically planned — clear responsibilities, an agile board — may be experienced very differently in social reality. Is the tool being used? Is the responsibility taken seriously? Does the process match how people actually interact?

Teams function well when the technical structure supports social behavior rather than hindering it. When processes are too rigid, resistance or withdrawal emerges. When they are too vague, uncertainty and misunderstandings arise. The interplay of technical and social must be continually rebalanced — ideally in dialogue with the team itself.

## Membership in the Team

Membership in a team almost always begins formally: through a role, a job description, an invitation. But whether someone actually _feels_ part of the team develops over time. Belonging is a feeling, not an administrative act. It grows through daily interaction: through trust, shared experiences, resonance.

Membership is not static. It changes subtly or abruptly. Someone withdraws, someone new arrives, a role shift changes a person's position in the team. Such changes never affect only the individual — they always affect the entire fabric: relationships shift, informal roles are redistributed, old conflicts may resurface.

This dynamic is especially sensitive because belonging is a fundamental need. Those who don't feel they belong show less initiative and often unconsciously take less responsibility. This is why team development must include talking about membership: who feels core, who feels peripheral? Who is included, who is overlooked?

## The Team and Its Environment

Teams never act completely independently. They always exist in relation to an "outside": the organization, leadership, customers, other teams. This environment provides mandates, sets goals, defines constraints — and often creates tensions: between adaptation and autonomy, between external loyalty and internal cohesion.

Some teams accept mandates without reflection and then struggle internally. Others protect themselves through isolation and lose connection. Part of every team development journey is consciously engaging with the environment: actively clarifying expectations, influencing conditions, raising ambiguities.

The flow of information between team and environment is also important: what is passed on, what is filtered? Who speaks for the team and with what legitimacy? These are often sites of hidden conflict and power dynamics. Making them visible is a crucial step in every team's development.

## Division of Labor

Division of labor is one of the great strengths of teamwork: complex tasks are better handled when different skills, perspectives, and responsibilities come together. But this also creates tension.

Technically, division of labor needs clear structures: who handles which tasks? How do handovers work? Which interfaces need coordination? The clearer these questions are answered, the more efficient collaboration runs — at least in theory.

Socially, however, division of labor is anything but simple. It raises questions of fairness, trust, influence, and visibility. Who feels overloaded, who under-challenged? Who gets recognition and who doesn't? How do we handle errors at interfaces?

## Team Dynamics as Process

Teams are not stable entities. They are in constant motion. New members come, others leave. Tasks change. Relationships evolve or harden. Teams develop because people develop and because external demands change. Team development means not just observing these movements but actively shaping them.

The larger a team grows, the more complex its dynamics become. According to Dunbar's theory, the natural upper limit of stable social relationships for teamwork lies between 5 and 15 people. The number of relationships (r) grows exponentially to the number of team members (n): **r = n × (n-1) ÷ 2**. In a team of 5, there are 10 relationships. At 12 members, that's already 66. With each new relationship, communication effort increases — along with the potential for misunderstandings, coordination problems, but also diversity, synergy, and growth.

Once a team has more than four members, it becomes increasingly likely that smaller groups will form, consciously or unconsciously. This isn't necessarily bad. Subgroups can be a strength: they enable parallel work streams, foster quick exchange in smaller circles, and make the team more adaptable when used consciously.
